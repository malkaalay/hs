Artist Story

From Survival To Music

I’m Loai Miqdad, known as Hicats Sam / HS. I’m a 23-year-old rapper, songwriter and visual storyteller from Gaza City, Palestine.

Before music became the main direction I wanted to follow, my life was built around software and database development. I studied the field, graduated with a diploma, and worked as a developer. Music was always there, but before the war most of my passion was connected to Arabic singing and music.

When music changed for me

The war changed the direction of my life. Our home was bombed, my family and I went through repeated displacement, and the things that once felt normal — a room, a laptop, electricity, internet, a place to work — became uncertain.

We were displaced to a school because we hoped it would be safer. Instead, nights could mean darkness, bombing, artillery, gunfire and drones. When the situation around the school became too dangerous, we were forced to leave again. During displacement I saw injured people, smoke, tanks, destroyed streets and bodies on the ground. Those memories later became part of the world behind songs like Bad Story.

When we reached the south, we lived in tents. That period also became the emotional ground for songs I was already beginning to write: Homeless, Lost Mom and Bad Story.

I lost almost everything that made creating easy, but I did not stop planning for music.

Why English rap

During our first displacement I saw MC Abdul, a young rapper from Gaza, performing in English and speaking about Gaza. I remember thinking: I am living this story too. Why shouldn’t I be one of the people telling it myself?

That moment pushed me to take English rap seriously. English did not replace my identity; it became a bridge. I wanted people who could not understand Arabic to still hear directly from someone living through fear, displacement, loss of home and the psychological pressure of war.

I started studying artists who made me think differently about storytelling and performance. NF influenced me through emotion and internal struggle. Eminem pushed me to study flow, rhyme, delivery and wordplay. Linkin Park showed me how anger, vulnerability and energy could exist in the same song.

I taught myself through YouTube, podcasts, interviews, vlogs and music. I would repeat words, record myself, listen back and try again. Learning English without a normal environment for practice was difficult, but it became part of the work.

People who helped me grow

I did not build this journey alone. Billy Salisbury from the UK helped me with pronunciation, accent, flow, delivery, singing, writing and confidence. His support has been consistent over time, not connected to only one song.

Garry Mc from The Kabin Studio in Ireland helped me understand rap more practically. He wrote 16 bars for me as an exercise and stayed with me step by step until I could perform all 16, working with me on rhythm, rhyme, entering the beat and finding the right flow.

In Gaza, Qism was my friend and producer. He gave me access to his studio, helped me record and produce, gave me his time without charging me, and believed in what I was trying to build. I also met Raji Al Jaru through this journey, another musician from Gaza, and we are now working together on new music.

FEAR

FEAR is my cover and reinterpretation of NF’s FEAR. I connected deeply with the original song, but I did not want to make a normal cover. I wanted to build a short music film around fear, memory, isolation, pressure and what war can do to the mind.

The production took around eight months. The entire video was filmed on an iPhone 16 Pro Max with friends who believed in the idea. There was no production company, large crew or cinema setup behind it.

I did not have my own laptop while making FEAR. I used a friend’s laptop after he finished his own work. I could not afford professional editing, so the project became part film and part classroom: I learned by cutting, changing the pacing, working on color, reshooting scenes and teaching myself new techniques until the video felt right.

FEAR taught me how far I could go with limited tools when the idea mattered enough.

I lost Qism during the war only a few months before FEAR was officially released on 19 July 2026. That loss was personal, but it also changed the practical side of my music because I lost the friend who had helped make recording and production possible when I could not afford normal studio costs.

What comes after FEAR

November 2026 update: I am developing STILL HUMAN, my first original cinematic rap album combining emotional English rap, orchestral sound and cinematic storytelling. It is not released yet; I am seeking producers, collaborators and production support. Since losing Qism — my close friend and the person who gave me access to a recording studio — as well as our home and my laptop, making music while living in a tent and struggling for power and internet has become a daily challenge. This album is about holding onto humanity and art through it all.

The biggest problem now is not ideas. I have songs, lyrics and visual concepts. The challenge is producing them.

My next original project is STILL HUMAN. It currently includes four songs: Homeless, about displacement and losing the feeling of home; Lost Mom, built from one of my biggest fears after our home was bombed — imagining what life would feel like if I lost my mother; Bad Story, based on a real period of displacement and survival; and Can You Hold Me, which includes a collaboration with Rita Mousi.

Separate from STILL HUMAN, I am also working on a cover of Linkin Park’s In the End with Raji Al Jaru and Rita Mousi. We are changing parts of the approach so the performance connects with our reality in Gaza while still respecting the original song.

What I want people to see

I want my music to carry Gaza with it, but I do not want to be reduced to only “an artist from a war.” I want people to see the artist too — the writing, performance, visual ideas, ambition and the sound I am still building.

I do not speak for everyone in Gaza. I speak about what I lived through. If that helps someone outside Gaza see Palestinians as people with individual stories, dreams, ideas and creative lives, then the music has done something important.

For me, FEAR was not the end. It was the beginning of the artist I am still trying to become.

Transparency: FEAR is based primarily on real-location footage, real performers and my own direction, filming and editing. Three very brief symbolic sequences use AI-generated imagery: the writing appearing on the wall, the door sequence and the piano sequence. They were used as artistic metaphors, not to recreate events or present fabricated documentary material.
